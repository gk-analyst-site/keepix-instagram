HOOK (first line of caption): Your keeper can't see the ball — here's how you coach them anyway.

CAPTION:
Late autumn. Training ends after dark, or a cup tie kicks off with the sun sitting just above the stand roof. The keeper is squinting, shifting weight, trying to find the ball in a wash of glare. This is a real coaching problem — and most sessions never address it.

Limited-visibility conditions change everything for a goalkeeper. Depth perception drops. Reaction windows shrink. The tendency is to freeze slightly before the dive — a tiny hesitation that costs the stop. Knowing why this happens lets you coach around it.

Start with set position. In low light, keepers often stand too tall, trying to lift their line of sight above the glare. Remind them: a lower base keeps the centre of gravity ready and actually helps them pick up flight earlier off the boot. Reinforce this during your normal footwork reps before the session goes live.

Crossing and high-ball work are where conditions bite hardest. When the ball comes out of a bright background, judgment of the flight's apex goes first. Train your keeper to read the server's body shape and the full run-up — not just the ball — so they're already moving before the flight confirms it. That habit is built in daylight; it pays off in the glare.

For distribution, ask your keeper where their target landmarks are. At dusk the near touchline, the six-yard box corners, and a teammate's shirt colour all shift. A short pre-session orientation — "find your three reference points tonight" — costs ninety seconds and sharpens awareness for the whole session.

You can't control the floodlights. You can control how prepared your keeper's decision-making is when those lights aren't enough.
