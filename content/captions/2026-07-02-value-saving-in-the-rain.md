CAPTION:
Wet conditions don't change the game — they amplify every technical flaw your keeper already has. Here's what we coach when the heavens open.

GRIP FIRST.
In dry conditions, goalkeepers get away with a loose hand shape at the moment of contact. In the rain, that margin disappears. Cue a firm, active grip through the ball — fingers spread, thumbs close, wrists locked just before impact. The ball will slide off a passive hand faster than you think. Train this before the session, not in the middle of a fumble.

CONTACT CHOICES CHANGE.
Your keeper's default contact point — the heel of the palm for power shots, the fingers for dipping deliveries — stays the same in principle, but the execution window shrinks. Shots that arrive with pace and swerve in the dry will skid low and fast on wet grass. Coach your keepers to get behind the ball earlier and let it travel into the hands rather than reaching at it. The punch is also a legitimate choice on crosses in heavy rain: cleaner, more controlled, less demand on grip.

FOOTWORK ON WET GRASS.
Short, choppy steps. This is the adjustment that gets left out of most GK sessions. Wet grass reduces traction and changes how quickly a keeper can push off into a dive. The set position still applies — weight forward, heels off the ground — but the recovery stride has to be tighter. Overstriding kills the weight transfer. Coach it by narrowing the cone gates on your footwork runs and asking your keeper to feel the difference.

ONE DRILL TO RUN TODAY.
Set up a central cone 3m off the line. Server plays varied deliveries — driven low, bouncing, clipped high — to corners in quick succession. Keeper must reset to the cone between each rep. In wet conditions, this builds the habit of controlled recovery steps rather than wide, sliding strides. Watch the feet first. The hands will follow.
