HOOK (first line of caption): The near post isn't a gamble — it's a guarantee your keeper has to own.

CAPTION:
Every shot conceded at the near post is a conversation in the changing room. Not because the keeper wasn't trying — but because the set position or the step pattern broke down before the ball was even struck.

Here's what we see most often: the keeper is slightly off their line, weight back on the heels, and the angle isn't properly closed. The shooter doesn't go far post. They don't need to.

Three things that protect the near post:

1. SET POSITION RELATIVE TO THE BALL — As the ball moves wider, the keeper tracks it. The near post side of the body should always feel like it's covering the shorter route. If the striker can see daylight between the keeper and the post, that's where they're going.

2. FORWARD WEIGHT TRANSFER — Heels are the enemy on near-post shots. The moment the player shapes to shoot, weight should already be transferring onto the front foot. It creates a split-second of readiness that changes what the keeper can reach.

3. THE SPLIT STEP — Don't wait flat-footed for the contact. A small, low split step as the shooter's foot meets the ball keeps the keeper explosive. Planted feet and near-post shots are an uncomfortable combination.

Drill it in isolation first — cones marking the near post line, coach serving driven shots from tight angles, keeper practising the weight shift before the serve lands. Make it automatic before you add pressure.

The far post is always the talking point. The near post is where games are decided.
