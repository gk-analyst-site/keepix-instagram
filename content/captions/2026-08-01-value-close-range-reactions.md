HOOK (first line of caption): The keeper who stays on their feet longest usually wins the close-range battle.

CAPTION:
At five metres or less, the shot is already on its way before your brain has finished processing the cross. You don't get to set. You don't get to think. All you have is shape, hand position, and how quickly you can move without falling over yourself.

Here are three principles we keep coming back to in close-range reaction work:

1. Stay tall as long as possible. Dropping to ground is a one-way door — once you're down, you can't recover to the second ball, the rebound, or the next shot. Weight on the balls of your feet, slight forward lean, hands in front of the body. Let the shot come to you before you commit.

2. Drive with the hands first. The hands lead, the body follows. If you're dipping a shoulder or lunging a knee first, you've already slowed your reaction down. Short, sharp hand punches toward the ball — think of it less as a dive and more as a strike.

3. Train the panic. Most close-range situations feel chaotic because we practise them when everything is controlled. Vary the delivery angle, add a second server, let the ball come off a wall or a mannequin at awkward height. The more cluttered the environment in training, the cleaner the keeper's decision-making gets under real pressure.

This is the kind of repetition that builds genuine reflexes — not luck, not athleticism alone, but patterned responses you've earned in the week before the match.
