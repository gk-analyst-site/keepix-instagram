CAPTION:
After 90 minutes, most of the noise has faded. What travels the whole game is language that means something — short, directional, agreed before kick-off.

Keeper communication isn't about volume. It's about three things: timing, vocabulary, and trust. If your defenders have to think about what you mean, you're already half a second late.

Build a shared vocabulary in training, not in the game. "Step" means the line pushes up. "Hold" means stay. "Away" means the winger is live and you're calling the cross. Five words. Every player knows them. You don't earn that in a warm-up — you earn it over weeks of repetition until it's automatic.

The moment to lead isn't always the obvious one. It's not just the back-post cross or the through ball. It's the quiet moment at 0-0 when your centre-back is drifting and you reset the line before the opponent even plays forward. That's when the best keepers talk — before the problem, not because of it.

One drill worth adding to your next session: play a rondo with your back four, keeper included, but the keeper can only communicate — no touches. Force them to read, organise, and lead with nothing but their voice. You'll find out very quickly how specific their language actually is.

Communication is a technical skill. Train it like one.
