HOOK (first line of caption): He was the last keeper I expected to still be playing football at 18.

CAPTION:
A wet Tuesday evening in February. Half the squad hadn't shown up. He was there — same
as always — but nothing was clicking. Footwork sluggish, decision-making half a second
late, set position collapsing the moment the ball moved. After training I almost pulled
him aside to have the conversation coaches dread.

I didn't.

Instead I spent the next week watching — not correcting. I stopped calling out every
misaligned foot and every hesitation. I let the session give him feedback instead of
me giving it. The moment I created some space between him and my voice, something
shifted. He started reading the drill. He started self-correcting.

What changed on my end was small but it mattered. I restructured the repetitions so
the consequence of a bad decision was immediate and obvious — not a word from me, just
the next ball arriving before he'd recovered position. The environment did the teaching.

He started turning up differently after that. Not because he became a different person.
Because the training finally matched the way he actually learned.

That keeper is still playing. He'll remind you of that if you give him half a chance.

I think about that February evening whenever I'm tempted to talk more and design less.
The session is the message.
