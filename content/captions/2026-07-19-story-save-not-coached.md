CAPTION:
It was one of those matches where you stop taking notes.
Late in the game. Opposition break. Ball fizzing across the box at an awkward height —
too high to smother, too low to claim cleanly. Nothing we'd ever specifically drilled.

Our keeper read it. Shifted her weight, stayed patient for half a beat longer than I would have coached,
and made a stop that isn't in any session plan I've written. Then jogged back to her line like it was nothing.

I stood there and realised: I had never taught her that.
She had learned it — through repetition, through failure, through the hours of work —
but the solve in that moment was entirely hers.

There's a version of coaching that tries to have an answer for everything.
A cue for every cross, a script for every 1v1. I've been that coach.
And moments like this are the reminder that over-coaching can quietly shrink the space
where a keeper learns to think for themselves.

Our job isn't to wire every response in advance.
It's to build the foundation — footwork, positioning, reading the game —
then trust the keeper to complete the picture.

She taught me more in three seconds than I taught her that entire week.
